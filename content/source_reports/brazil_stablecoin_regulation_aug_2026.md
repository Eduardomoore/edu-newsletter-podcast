# Brazil Tightened Stablecoin Rules. Volume Grew Right Through Them Anyway.
Brazil's stablecoin regulation, real trade settlement and dollarization demand, and why
Circle, Samsung and BNY are building the same rails.
August 2026 · Written by Eduardo Moore

## Overview

Brazil spent the past year building the most credible regulated crypto framework in Latin
America, then tightened how one specific settlement corridor can use stablecoins. Adoption
didn't slow down for it. Wall Street spent the same few months doing the opposite: Circle
got a federal bank charter, BNY put USDC in custody, Samsung put it on 241 million phones.

This report covers the segments actually moving stablecoin volume in Brazil today — trade
settlement, dollarization, and cross-border shopping — and who's building for each one.

A few weeks ago, in the H1 LatAm fintech report, Trend #3 was stablecoins moving from
experiment to core infrastructure. Brazil's central bank proved the point by tightening its
grip on them in the same quarter.

The split screen: Bitcoin is down 49% from its October peak, trading near $64,000. Daily
spot trading volume across major exchanges has fallen 70% since January. Every crypto
headline right now says winter. Meanwhile Brazilians bought $2.6 billion in stablecoins in
May alone, up 158% year over year. Brazil sent $6.7 billion abroad in stablecoins in Q1,
double what it sent a year earlier, exporters and importers among the biggest reasons why.
Félix Pago crossed $3 billion turning WhatsApp messages into USDC into pesos at the
US-Mexico border. Samsung put a USDC balance next to the boarding pass on 241 million
Galaxy phones.

Speculative trading is cooling off fast. The plumbing underneath it — the part moving real
dollars for real reasons — is accelerating. Brazil's central bank just tightened the
rulebook around that plumbing, in the same stretch that Wall Street decided to own the
plumbing outright.

## Section 01 — Stablecoin Purchases in Brazil Rose 158% in a Year, Though Headline Volume Overstates Real Usage

Brazil received $318.8 billion in on-chain crypto value over the past year, close to a third
of everything that touched Latin America, which makes it the region's crypto market more
than a market within it. BCB president Gabriel Galipolo has said directly that around 90% of
that flow is stablecoin movement, not Bitcoin, not altcoins. Since 2019, stablecoins account
for roughly $219 billion, 71.7%, of all crypto volume Brazilians have declared to the tax
authority. USDT still owns the corridor: 88.7% of that declared stablecoin flow runs through
Tether, even with Circle pushing hard on the institutional side.

The growth rate is the part that should reframe how you read the regulatory story. Official
BCB data on registered platforms shows Brazilians bought $2.63 billion in stablecoins in May
2026 alone, up 158% year over year, and $2.54 billion in June versus $1.48 billion a year
earlier. Adoption accelerated straight through the rules tightening around it.

Headline stablecoin volume overstates real economic usage almost everywhere. Of roughly $35
trillion in global on-chain stablecoin volume, only about $390 billion, close to 1%,
represents actual payments rather than trading, internal transfers between a platform's own
wallets, or treasury shuffling. The Brazil numbers in this report stay close to genuine
end-user activity — trade settlement, consumer dollarization, cross-border shopping — not
raw on-chain totals.

## Section 02 — Trade Settlement and Dollarization Carry Brazil's Real Stablecoin Volume

Trade settlement and dollarization carry the real volume in Brazil today. Freelancer and
remote-worker income is a smaller, earlier-stage third.

### Trade Settlement — Brazil's Biggest Stablecoin Segment, and Its Least Penetrated

The customer: Brazilian trade-facing SMEs, câmbio brokers and eFX providers moving payment
for goods crossing the border. A business segment that behaves like consumer plumbing — high
frequency, time-sensitive, cost-obsessed.

The pain: a traditional SWIFT wire can take up to five days to settle, and cargo sits at
port waiting for payment confirmation the whole time, generating real demurrage cost on top
of the delay. Traditional câmbio adds FX spread and bank fees on top, and only clears during
banking hours in two different time zones. Then in 2026 the government added a layer of its
own dysfunction: it proposed extending the 3.5% IOF tax to stablecoin operations by decree,
the industry pushed back, the government shelved it, then revived a version of it in May
through a technical reclassification. That uncertainty pushed volume toward stablecoins
rather than away from them — predictability turned out to matter more than the tax question
itself.

The fix, and who's building it: BCB tracks $6.7 billion in total stablecoin operations sent
abroad by Brazilians in Q1 2026, double the same quarter in 2025, with stablecoins now
accounting for 98% of all crypto Brazilians send abroad. That figure covers all outbound
stablecoin flow, not an isolated import/export line item. Reporting on it points to
importers, exporters and câmbio brokers as major drivers cutting costs versus SWIFT, but
there's no official breakdown by purpose. VCX Finance built its entire pitch around the
trade use case specifically: Pix in, USDT or USDC settlement, positioned as the modern
alternative to SWIFT, running inside BCB's eFX compliance framework. Transfero runs a
parallel play issuing BRZ, its own real-pegged stablecoin, plus Transfero Checkout for
merchant settlement.

The trend, and how early this actually is: even if every dollar of that $6.7 billion were
trade-related, which it isn't, Brazil moves roughly $160 billion a quarter in total goods
trade. That puts stablecoin penetration in Brazilian trade settlement at a ceiling of around
4%, and the real number is almost certainly lower. Stablecoins have barely scratched
Brazilian trade finance, and the upside case is a market in the hundreds of billions, not
the low single-digit billions currently moving. The catch is that Resolution 561 tightens
the exact channel, eFX settlement, that's driven this early growth. The read from Daniel de
Paiva Gomes, a Brazilian crypto regulation lawyer, is that the broader cross-border virtual
asset category under Resolutions 519 through 521 stays fully operative; only the narrow
eFX-provider-to-foreign-counterparty settlement leg closes. If that migration path holds and
stays cheap and fast, this segment has more room to grow than any other one in this report.
If it doesn't, October 1 is where the growth story stalls.

### Dollarization — Brazilians Are Buying USD Exposure Even Though It Costs Them 9 to 10 Points of Yield

The customer: Brazilians who want USD exposure without a foreign bank account, a US
address, or a five-figure minimum. Mercado Bitcoin alone logged 40,000 new stablecoin buyers
in 2025, average ticket size up 190%, concentrated in the 35 to 44 age bracket. That's
Brazilians protecting savings, not speculating.

The pain: real depreciation and inflation memory make BRL-only savings feel exposed, and the
traditional fix — a Wise or Nomad style global account — still means real paperwork and
slower onboarding than opening an app. There's a real cost to this protection worth being
honest about. Brazil's Selic sits at 14%, and CDI-linked accounts pay close to that in
reais. Every real moved into a dollar position gives up most of that yield, but protects
against currency depreciation in a presidential election year. That gap is the actual
competitive pressure these products are underwriting, not a footnote.

The fix, and who's building it: ARQ, formerly DollarApp, lets users deposit via Pix and
convert straight to USDC, no US address, SSN or ITIN required, approval automated in most
cases, and pays tiered yield on the USDC balance itself, 2% to 4.5% a year depending on
account tier, alongside up to 120% of CDI on any reais left in the account. Nubank offers a
comparable rewards program paying roughly 4% a year on USDC holdings, conditioned on minimum
balance and active participation. Mercado Pago pays cashback in Meli Dólar, Mercado Livre's
own dollar-pegged stablecoin, on credit card purchases for Meli+ subscribers, redeemable at
Mercado Livre or sold for reais instantly with no fees. Avenue rolled out USDC to its
Brazilian client base in November 2025. OKX Pay and Crypto.com run the same stablecoin
account model as an alternative to Wise, Revolut, Nomad and C6 Global's traditional
fiat-dollar accounts. Circle reported roughly $79 billion in USDC circulation as of March
2026, audited monthly by Deloitte, which is the trust anchor the whole category leans on.

The trend: up, and structurally sticky, because it's solving a savings problem, not a
trading one, and the yield gap against CDI hasn't been enough to slow it down. USDT still
dominates the corridor at 88.7% of flow, but watch the October 30 VASP licensing deadline
below — it will decide which of these apps can keep serving this segment legally.

### Freelancer Income — Real Demand, But Brazil-Specific Volume Isn't Published Yet

The customer: Brazilian freelancers and contractors billing foreign clients directly or
through platforms like Upwork and Deel.

The pain: wire withdrawal from platforms like Upwork to a Brazilian bank carries a flat $30
fee, on top of platform withdrawal fees running 5% to 20%.

The fix: Deel processed $250 million in crypto payouts globally in 2025 and launched formal
USDC payroll through its BVNK partnership in 2026. USDC-to-Pix conversion went live in
Brazil this year through products like Wayex.

## Section 03 — Resolution 561 Closes One Settlement Corridor, Not the Stablecoin Market

Resolution BCB 561, published April 30 and effective October 1, rewrites how Brazil's eFX
international payment service can settle. From October 1, payments between a Brazilian eFX
provider and its foreign counterparty must run exclusively through a registered câmbio
operation or a non-resident real account. Stablecoins are explicitly excluded as a
settlement instrument in that specific corridor.

The nuance worth getting right, because most of the initial coverage overstated it: Thiago
Amaral, a partner at Barcellos Tucunduva who specializes in payments and crypto, has been
direct about the scope — the resolution does not ban stablecoins in Brazil. What it does is
stop the eFX provider itself from using virtual assets to settle with its foreign
counterparty. Client custody of stablecoins stays legal. It's decoupled from the FX
operation, not eliminated.

Why now, specifically: the stated rationale across every primary source is traceability and
anti-money-laundering, and it's not manufactured urgency. Brazil's "CPI das Bets"
congressional inquiry into the betting industry found CPFs belonging to children, deceased
people and falsified data attached to remittances flowing through exactly this kind of
unsupervised corridor. Resolution 561 isn't a standalone reaction to that — it extends a
normative process the BCB signaled back in mid-2024. But it lands on top of a real fraud
pattern the regulator can point to, not a hypothetical one.

This is also the part worth being skeptical about. It's one piece of a sequence, not an
isolated event. Resolutions 519, 520 and 521 built the VASP authorization regime in November
2025. Resolution 561 closes the eFX settlement leg in October. Resolution 580, from July,
folds virtual asset providers into the same prudential regime as securities brokerages. And
in early August, the BCB added a 24-hour cautionary hold on virtual asset transfers above
$10,000 headed to foreign exchanges or self-custodied wallets, effective January 2027. Four
resolutions in nine months is a regulator moving with real conviction, or a regulator moving
faster than the market can absorb, depending on who you ask.

Not everyone is convinced it was the right call. Industry associations ABToken and ABCripto
came out against the August hold specifically with a sharp technical argument: a
fixed-duration hold doesn't distinguish a legitimate transaction from an illicit one, it
just delays both equally, and it's trivial to route around by splitting a transfer into
smaller pieces. Federal deputy Júlia Zanatta filed a legislative motion in August to suspend
that resolution outright, backed by the free-market lobby Instituto Livre Mercado, arguing
the BCB overstepped its authority. Separately, a payments company called Corpag won a court
injunction earlier this year to keep operating after the BCB denied its license, which was
enough of a shock to the system that a group of banks and fintechs sent the BCB a joint
letter asking the judiciary to defer to the regulator's technical judgment going forward.
None of this has stopped anything yet. All of it tells you the ground under this regulatory
push is less settled than a resolution number makes it look.

The deadline that actually matters operationally is October 30, when every virtual asset
service provider currently running in Brazil has to file for authorization. An estimated 120
providers serve the market today, most without formal licensing. Minimum capital
requirements run from roughly $2.1 million to $7.2 million depending on the license
category, and foreign firms serving Brazil from offshore shells get 270 days to bring
operations onshore. That bar is going to filter the market hard.

## Section 04 — The US and Brazil Are Institutionalizing Stablecoins at the Same Time, From Opposite Starting Points

While Brazil was tightening one corridor, the largest financial institutions in the world
spent June and July racing to own the plumbing outright.

Circle received final approval from the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency on
July 10 to run a national trust bank, Circle National Trust, bringing USDC's issuer under
direct federal banking supervision for the first time. Nineteen days earlier, BNY, the
world's largest custody bank at $59.4 trillion in assets, made USDC the first stablecoin on
its Digital Asset Custody platform, letting institutional clients mint and redeem directly
through BNY rather than routing through Circle separately. On July 16, Marex began accepting
USDC as initial margin collateral for regulated derivatives, with Coinbase providing the
custody and conversion infrastructure underneath. Circle's Claire Ching called it collateral
moving at internet speed; Coinbase's Liz Martin said she expects the model to extend across
more clearinghouses as the market moves toward always-on collateral. Samsung, at Galaxy
Unpacked on July 22, demoed native stablecoin support inside Samsung Wallet, the app already
sitting on 241 million Galaxy phones shipped in 2025, showing USDC with send, receive and
top-up functions. No launch date, no confirmed issuer partnership, no confirmed country
list — still the largest distribution surface stablecoins have ever been shown on.

Put side by side with Brazil, the instinct looks opposite. Look closer and it's the same
move from two different starting points. Both are institutionalizing the same asset class.
The US is pulling stablecoins into the banking system — chartering the issuer, custodying
the token at a 240-year-old trust bank, accepting it as derivatives collateral. Brazil is
pulling the companies that move stablecoins into a bank-like regulatory perimeter — capital
requirements, licensing, AML controls, the same treatment its securities brokerages already
carry. Neither government is trying to make stablecoins go away. Both are deciding who gets
to be trusted with them.

## Section 05 — The $2 to $7 Million Capital Bar Will Consolidate Brazil's Stablecoin Market by October 30

Brazil is picking who gets to run the pipes. OKX, Mercado Bitcoin, Bitso, Binance Brazil,
dLocal and Transfero can absorb a $2 to $7 million capital requirement and a
compliance build-out. The roughly 120 smaller and informal operators serving today's
exporter, freelancer and remittance flows through side-door eFX arrangements mostly can't,
not by October 30. That's consolidation, and it favors exactly the players already big
enough to have shown up in this newsletter before.

dLocal's stablecoin infrastructure lead, Marcelo Dutilh, has framed the broader opportunity
as emerging markets being where the next wave of digital consumers is coming from, while
moving money in and out of those economies stays genuinely complex. Brazil just made that
complexity a compliance problem, which is a trade the largest players are built to win and
the smallest are not.

### The Circumvention Risk: Tighter Rules Could Push Volume Into Self-Custody the BCB Can't See

Here's the case against the argument above: every one of these resolutions assumes tighter
friction produces more visibility. ABToken and ABCripto's critique cuts at exactly that
assumption — a fixed 24-hour hold doesn't catch bad actors, it delays everyone equally, and
anyone actually trying to launder funds splits the transfer below the $10,000 threshold and
moves on. Meanwhile, the freelancer getting paid by a US client, or the small exporter
waiting on a shipment, absorbs the friction with zero corresponding security benefit.
Regulation aimed at catching sophisticated bad actors that mainly slows down unsophisticated
good ones is a real design failure, not a rounding error.

There's a second-order risk: merchant acceptance of stablecoins in Brazil is still low, only
37% of crypto users report having paid in a store with digital assets despite saying they'd
like to, largely because blockchain rails don't talk to legacy point-of-sale systems. That
means most of today's stablecoin activity already routes through exchanges and licensed
on-ramps where the BCB can see it. Push enough friction into that regulated layer, and some
share of volume doesn't disappear — it moves into self-custody and offshore rails the BCB
genuinely cannot see. A regulator optimizing for traceability can end up producing less of
it. This is the scenario worth watching most closely through 2027.

Where this reverses: if Brazil calibrates enforcement toward risk-based thresholds instead
of blanket holds, and if the October 30 licensing wave brings enough of the current 120
providers into the regulated perimeter rather than pushing them offshore, the traceability
goal and the growth story stay compatible. If it doesn't, expect the gap between BCB's
declared volume and on-chain estimates from firms like TRM Labs and Chainalysis to widen —
the clearest sign the crackdown pushed activity underground instead of formalizing it.

## Section 06 — Compliance Cost Is Becoming LatAm Fintech's New Investment Moat

For capital allocators in LatAm fintech infrastructure: the licensing wave is the resolution
itself. Capital requirements this steep favor platforms that already have a balance sheet
over point solutions built on regulatory arbitrage. That's the same pattern H1 2026 showed
in credit infrastructure — scaled operators pulling ahead of venture-stage ones — just
showing up a cycle later in crypto.

For anything insurance-adjacent with cross-border exposure: watch how claims settlement and
reinsurance flows start touching this rail. Faster, cheaper cross-border settlement is
exactly the kind of infrastructure insurance has needed for international claims payouts for
years.

## Section 07 — What Keeps Growing Regardless of the Next Resolution

Three demand forces will keep pushing money into Brazilian stablecoins no matter which
resolution is currently in effect, and they're a better guide to 2027 than the compliance
calendar is.

Trade settlement has the most room to run of anything in this report. By a generous
estimate, stablecoins currently touch a low single-digit share of Brazil's roughly $640
billion in annual goods trade. That's a market they've barely entered, not one they've
captured, and the reasons companies started using them — SWIFT's five-day settlement and
unpredictable câmbio spreads — aren't going away after October 1. Expect the existing flow
to show up inside licensed VASP rails instead of informal eFX arrangements rather than
disappearing. The players to watch are the ones building purpose-built trade-settlement
products — Mercado Bitcoin, VCX Finance, Bitso, Transfero — rather than treating this as a
side effect of their consumer exchange business.

Dollarization keeps growing even though the yield math argues against it. Selic sits at 14%,
and CDI accounts pay close to that in reais. ARQ's best USDC tier tops out around 4.5% a
year; Nubank pays roughly 4% conditionally. Anyone doing the math is giving up 9 to 10
points of annual return to hold dollars instead of reais, and the segment is growing anyway,
because it's underwriting currency protection, not chasing yield. Expect yield to become a
default feature on these accounts rather than a premium add-on.

The real consumer edge is cross-border online shopping. Most Brazilians earn in reais and
spend in reais, Pix already covers that job for free, and there's no real reason for someone
buying groceries to reach for a stablecoin instead. The edge shows up when Brazilians buy
from foreign sellers online — AliExpress, Shein, Amazon, and increasingly Mercado Livre's
own cross-border listings — a category growing fast inside Brazil's roughly $64 billion
e-commerce market. A traditional Brazilian card on a foreign-currency purchase carries a
3.5% IOF plus a card-issuer spread that typically runs 4% to 7%, a combined markup that
regularly passes 10%. Stablecoins currently sit outside Brazil's câmbio rules entirely, so a
USDC-funded card carries neither charge. Crypto.com's Global Visa card and Picnic, a
Brazilian product marketed specifically for AliExpress and Shein purchases, are already
selling exactly this. One financial educator quoted in the press estimated he saved around
15% on a trip funding a crypto card with stablecoins instead of a traditional credit card.
That's a measurable price difference, a much better bet than a habit shift. It's also the
most fragile edge in this report: it exists only because stablecoins aren't classified as a
câmbio instrument today, and that's the exact classification gap the government has already
tried to close twice this year.

One more thing worth watching separately: B3 is building Brazil's next settlement layer, and
the central bank isn't the one doing it. Drex, Brazil's CBDC, stalled out on privacy, cost
and viability concerns. B3, the stock exchange, stepped into that vacuum with its own
real-pegged stablecoin, B3RL, launching within months, with tokenized stocks and fixed
income trading on top of it starting in early 2027. If B3RL becomes the standard settlement
asset for tokenized securities, that's a bigger structural shift than anything in Resolution
561.

## Wrapping Up

1. Brazil isn't banning stablecoins, it's licensing who can move them. Resolution 561 closes
   one settlement corridor, the eFX leg, not the asset class. The October 30 VASP deadline is
   the real filter, and it favors scaled operators over the roughly 120 smaller players
   serving the market informally today.

2. The growth is real, and it's accelerating through the crackdown. Brazilians bought $2.6
   billion in stablecoins in May, up 158% year over year. That undercuts any simple story
   where regulation and adoption move in opposite directions.

3. Trade settlement is early-innings, not saturated. Brazil sent $6.7 billion abroad in
   stablecoins in Q1, doubling year over year, but even generously that's a ceiling of
   roughly 4% of Brazil's quarterly goods trade. Whether this becomes a real share of the
   country's $640 billion annual trade flow depends on whether the October 1 migration to
   licensed VASP rails stays as cheap and fast as the channel it replaces.

4. The circumvention risk is real. Friction without risk-based calibration doesn't catch
   sophisticated actors, it pushes ordinary volume into self-custody and offshore rails the
   BCB can't see. Watch whether declared volume and on-chain estimates start diverging
   through 2027.

5. The US and Brazil are running the same institutionalization play from opposite ends.
   Circle's bank charter, BNY's custody expansion, Marex's collateral rails, and Samsung's
   241 million phones all say the same thing Brazil's licensing wave says: stablecoins are
   moving from experiment to infrastructure everywhere at once. Brazil just got there first,
   and is now finding out what infrastructure-grade regulation actually costs the informal
   layer underneath it.

The October 30 licensing wave decides which of today's roughly 120 providers survive as the
pipes. Whether trade settlement, dollarization, cross-border shopping and institutional
tokenization keep growing through that filter depends less on the BCB's next resolution and
more on whether Brazilian consumers and companies keep facing the same underlying problems —
currency instability, slow cross-border rails, and a card system that taxes foreign
purchases stablecoins currently don't — that stablecoins are already solving for them today.
