## Chapter: Agentic Commerce — AI Can Pay. Letting It Pay Alone Is the Hard Part.

Third trend, and this is the one I want to spend some time on: agentic commerce.

Think of it in three steps. In 2025, AI could help you shop. In 2026, AI can pay. The 2027 question is: can AI pay when you're not there?

Shopping-related generative AI use grew 35% between February and November 2025. But most of that activity still sits around discovery, research, and comparison. The shift now underway is from agent-assisted commerce, where AI helps a person decide what to buy, to agentic commerce, where software executes the purchase itself.

The first use cases are the easy ones. Groceries, household products, subscriptions, other low-ticket purchases where the cost of a mistake is limited. Roughly $375 billion of US e-commerce spending falls into that first wave of categories suited to agent-led purchasing. And a much larger pool could become agent-assisted over time.

LatAm is already part of this transition. But different companies are solving very different parts of the transaction.

Yuno is connecting merchants to AI shopping agents. Its Agentic Commerce product can expose merchant catalogs to platforms like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, and Copilot. It provides agent-compatible checkout endpoints. Then it routes the payment through the merchant's existing payment providers. Yuno doesn't need to become the merchant's PSP. It makes the merchant's current commerce stack accessible to agents.

Pomelo is working from the issuer side. In 2026, a Mastercard Agent Pay test used a Pomelo-issued, tokenized card to complete a purchase initiated through ChatGPT. Pomelo handled the credential and authorization infrastructure behind the transaction. But the disclosed flow still included the user confirming before the purchase went through.

DEUNA is using agents inside the merchant's payment operation. Its Athia product analyzes individual transactions and decides which processor to use, how to build the authorization request, and whether to retry a decline. That's agentic decision-making inside payment processing. Not an AI shopping agent buying on a consumer's behalf.

And dLocal is working closer to the authorization standard itself. It's worked with Google on the Agent Payments Protocol, known as AP2, which is designed to let agents carry verifiable evidence of what a consumer authorized, across different payment methods. Separately, dLocal's dMoRe merchant-of-record product handles local tax, billing, regulatory, and compliance requirements for companies selling into emerging markets. Two different problems. But both remove constraints an agent would hit trying to transact across markets.

The card networks are even further along. Visa and Santander have run controlled end-to-end agentic purchases in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Mexico, and Uruguay. And Mastercard has completed live Agent Pay transactions in the region with banks and processors including Itaú, Galicia, Bancolombia, Banamex, Santander, and Pomelo.

So an AI agent can already start a card purchase and get it authorized.

That does not mean authentication is solved.

Many online card payments already go through with no OTP or biometric challenge, because the issuer's fraud engine sees them as low-risk. The interesting case is what happens when the issuer does want extra authentication.

Today, that means an SMS code, banking-app approval, a passkey, or a biometric check. All of those assume the cardholder is present. An AI agent can't legitimately open your banking app, scan your face, or type in the code sent to your phone just because you once told it: buy me a television.

So the technical problem isn't how to remove authentication. It's how to authenticate the mandate, instead of re-authenticating the human at the moment of purchase.

Google's AP2 proposes one version of this. You authenticate upfront and sign a mandate defining what the agent is allowed to do. Buy a certain product. Stay under a maximum price. Use a particular payment method. Transact before a certain date. Later, the agent presents cryptographic evidence that the purchase falls within those instructions.

And that creates an important distinction. A $20 grocery order that an issuer approves without friction is relatively easy. Even a purchase an AI agent starts, but the customer confirms at checkout, still keeps the human in the loop.

The harder test sounds like this. "If this MacBook falls below $2,000 at any authorized retailer in the next 30 days, buy it with my Visa."

You give that instruction once. Three weeks later, at 3 AM, the condition is met. The agent picks the merchant and completes a $1,950 transaction. And the issuer considers it risky enough that a normal e-commerce purchase would have required step-up authentication.

Can the agent's signed mandate satisfy that requirement, without waking you up for Face ID, an OTP, or banking-app approval?

I couldn't find a publicly disclosed, scaled LatAm deployment showing that full flow for a materially high-value or high-risk consumer purchase. That's the remaining gap.

Yuno can connect the agent to the merchant. Pomelo can issue and process the credential. DEUNA can optimize the authorization path. dLocal and Google are working on how your instructions travel with the transaction. Visa and Mastercard are building agent-aware network rails.

The pieces are there. What still has to mature is the trust mechanism. The one that lets an issuer tell the difference between an agent legitimately using authority you granted earlier, and software attempting a card-not-present transaction while you're absent.

That distinction will matter far more in 2027 than whether an AI can technically press the checkout button.

## Chapter: Stablecoins Have Already Scaled in Latin America, Country by Country Rather Than as One Regional Story

Fourth trend: digital assets. And this is the one where the report gets Brazil wrong. We'll get there.

First, the global picture. Digital asset players make up 15% of global fintech revenue and 23% of equity funding. That's inside an asset class that's grown to roughly $3 trillion in crypto, $300 billion in stablecoins, and $30 billion in tokenized real-world assets. But past crypto itself, scaled use cases stay rare. Of stablecoin holdings, roughly 65% are tied to crypto trading. 25% to dollar access in emerging markets. And only 10% to real-economy payments. Cross-border stablecoin corridors work in specific emerging markets, and run into resistance in the largest ones: India, China, and Brazil among them.

B C G's view is that the more credible near-term path to scale is asset tokenization, not stablecoin payments. And that it won't scale evenly. Money market instruments, commodity funds, alternatives, and securitized debt look best positioned. Precisely where existing infrastructure is already costly, fragmented, or illiquid. And none of it scales without market utilities, regulators, issuers, and buy-side participants moving together.

Now zoom into Latin America. The global pattern turns out to be several different local stories, not one regional trend.

Mexico is the dollar-access and remittance case, even though the domestic rail, SPEI, already works well. Bitso Business alone handles roughly 10% of payouts in the US-Mexico corridor. And stablecoins made up 36% of crypto purchases there in the first half of 2025, against $62.5 billion in total remittances in 2025.

Brazil shows the same global split, just concentrated in commerce instead of trading. Pix solved domestic payments. Stablecoins now represent roughly 90% of Brazil's crypto transaction volume. And Brazil sent $6.7 billion abroad in stablecoins in Q1 2026, doubling year over year, on B2B trade settlement and contractor payroll.

Colombia mixes cross-border use with dollar access. 99% of Colombian-peso-funded exchange purchases flow straight into stablecoins. And Littio's WhatsApp interface makes a digital dollar or euro feel like a normal chat balance, not a crypto product.

Argentina remains a savings and dollar-access market. It now operates under a managed exchange-rate band, not the old peso-collapse story. And stablecoins represent more than half of exchange purchases, as the default way to hold dollar exposure.

Bolivia is the sharpest scarcity case. After the country lifted restrictions on virtual-asset transactions in 2024, USDT became the practical dollar proxy. Importers use it to pay foreign suppliers. Consumers fund cards and QR payments with it. Freelancers hold cross-border income in it. In a market where physical or bank dollars are genuinely hard to get.

And Uruguay is the outlier. Adoption there is infrastructure-led, not crisis-driven. Regulatory clarity and stronger institutional participation are pulling stablecoins into commerce. Not FX scarcity.

So you can split emerging-market stablecoin adoption into three patterns. Crisis-driven, in Argentina and Bolivia. Payments-and-commerce-driven, in Brazil, Colombia, and Mexico. And infrastructure-led, in Uruguay.

But the operating model underneath all six is the same. Fiat in, a stablecoin in the middle, fiat out.

A company can take Mexican pesos, convert to USDC or USDT, move that digital dollar across a blockchain, and convert to Colombian pesos on the other side. Neither party ever touches a wallet address or picks a chain. The jobs underneath that sandwich vary. A migrant sending money home. A freelancer holding foreign income before converting to Pix. An SME importer timing supplier payments. A regional treasury bridging fragmented local accounts. A saver protecting value. An investor reaching a tokenized asset.

And there's real room for better infrastructure to matter. The World Bank put the average cost of sending $200 into Latin America and the Caribbean at 5.72% in Q1 2025.

What none of that infrastructure removes are the adoption barriers B C G's global averages gloss over. And they split cleanly between consumers and businesses.

On the consumer side, the main barrier is trust. 45% named fraud or scams as a concern. 41% worried about the safety of their money. 38% about costs or hidden fees. 35% about acceptance. 35% about trust in the provider. And 34% about regulation and oversight. Meanwhile, only 12% said they were familiar with stablecoins at all.

On the business side, the blockers land in the same place by a different route. Compliance and sanctions screening. Liquidity requirements. FX execution costs. Local payout and off-ramp reliability. Treasury controls. Accounting and reconciliation. And counterparty, issuer, and reserve risk. A finance team adopts a rail once every function around the token is solved.

## Chapter: Brazil's Rules, and the Tokenization Play

Now, Brazil. Because this is where the report's mechanism is off.

Brazil's central bank is clearly tightening regulation. But here's what the rule actually does. Resolution BCB 561 was published on April 30, 2026, and takes effect on October 1, 2026. As of this writing, it isn't in force yet. And its prohibition is narrow. It stops an eFX provider from using virtual assets to settle payments with its foreign counterparty. Settlement has to go through a registered FX operation or a non-resident real account.

Thiago Amaral, a payments and crypto partner at Barcellos Tucunduva, has been explicit: this is not a general ban. Client custody of stablecoins stays legal. It's decoupled from the FX operation, not eliminated. And unauthorized eFX providers have until May 31, 2027 to file for authorization.

Meanwhile, adoption accelerated straight through the tightening. Brazilians bought $2.63 billion in stablecoins in May 2026, up 158% year over year, per BCB data on registered platforms. Brazil sent $6.7 billion abroad in stablecoins in Q1 2026, double the prior year. And stablecoins account for 98% of all crypto Brazilians send abroad.

Now, the tokenization half of the trend. Tokenized real-world assets will scale before stablecoin payments, reaching roughly 16% of investable real-world assets by 2035, with commodity funds and money market instruments leading.

And Brazil is running that play without the central bank. Drex stalled on privacy and cost. So B3, Brazil's stock exchange, stepped in with its own stablecoin pegged to the real, with tokenized stocks and fixed income trading on top of it from early 2027. Around it, the securitization plumbing is consolidating. Celcoin, a Brazilian banking-infrastructure provider, acquired the securitization specialist Vert. And Laqus, a Brazilian securities registrar, acquired eStar Finance to enter tokenized securities.

Outside Brazil, the same progression is showing up as investment products, not payments. TOHKN's tokenized US equities and ETF program in El Salvador. Lulo Bank's gold exposure through Lulo X in Colombia. Both start from a customer who already holds digital dollars and now wants to put them to work.

Once the first job, moving or holding money, is solved, the second job shows up on its own. What can I do with that money once it's there?

## Chapter: The Regulatory Gap Between Banks and Fintechs Closed in LatAm Before It Closed in the US

Fifth trend: the regulatory gap between banks and fintechs.

Globally, it's narrowing. US federal bank charter and new-bank applications went up more than five times from 2024 to 2025. Revolut, Nubank, Coinbase, and Ripple all applied. B C G's warning is that charters carry obligations alongside the advantages. They should only be pursued by firms already operating economically like a bank.

Latin America ran this play first. And it's further into the consequences.

Nu México began full commercial banking operations on August 6, 2026. Roughly $7 billion in assets, 15 million clients, and a $1.1 billion initial capital injection. That closes a multi-year SOFIPO-to-bank arc, backed by a $4.2 billion investment plan through 2030.

Nequi began operating on September 1 under its own financing-company license, inside Grupo Cibest, rather than as a Bancolombia business line. That moves around 27 million users under direct supervision.

Plata, the Mexican digital bank, secured authorization as a financing company in Colombia, its first market outside Mexico. And it's awaiting CNBV approval for a brokerage, targeting December 2026.

Kapital, a Mexican SME neobank, opened its Colombian license process. And Revolut received organizational authorization in Peru, with launch targeted for late 2027 to early 2028.

So here's the takeaway. Compliance capacity is becoming the moat. And it favors whoever already has a balance sheet. A license is now a capital requirement as much as a permission. Nu México's $1.1 billion injection is capital that can't chase growth. The CNBV's Basel III-style liquidity rules for Sofipos phase in 80% to 100% coverage between 2028 and 2030. That raises the cost of being small in Mexico. And Brazil's VASP regime, short for Virtual Asset Service Provider, sets a capital bar in the $2.1 million to $7.2 million range.

Then there's Chile. Together with China, Hong Kong, and the GCC, it's a market where conditions disadvantage fintech challengers relative to incumbents. Mercado Pago is still waiting on CMF approval for a credit card. The central bank reopened prepaid-card liquidity rules. Fintoc, a Chilean open-finance startup, had to become a non-bank card issuer to get moving. And Chilean SME lender Xepelin's down round remains the cautionary tale of the cycle.

## Chapter: Neobanks Go Full-Stack: Expanding Geographically and Climbing the Product Ladder

Sixth trend: neobanks going full-stack.

Neobanks are starting to look more and more like the banks they set out to disrupt. They're extending from payments, deposits, debit, and FX into secured and unsecured lending, investing, insurance, telecom, and wealth. Shifting from single-product challengers into full financial platforms. The objective is owning more of each customer's financial life, and becoming the primary financial relationship.

Start with the US push. Nubank received OCC conditional approval in January 2026. But FDIC deposit insurance and a Federal Reserve account are still outstanding. And the company's own timeline points to Nubank N.A. opening in 2027.

Nubank didn't wait. On September 10, 2026, it launched US consumer accounts anyway, through Lead Bank, an FDIC-insured partner. That's the same banking-as-a-service route Chime and Current used to get to market. Alongside it came Nu Global, a multi-currency account holding USDC and EURC. Legally, it's operating in the US as a fintech, not a bank, until the charter itself clears.

Revolut is running the same three-regulator gauntlet a few weeks behind. It received its own OCC conditional approval on September 3, 2026, for Revolut Bank US, after filing in March. FDIC insurance and a Federal Reserve sign-off are still outstanding. The OCC is requiring at least $95 million of initial capitalization. And the company is targeting a first-half-2027 launch.

That approval also lands right after the OCC denied Wise a national trust charter in July, over an unresolved AML consent order. So Revolut clearing the same bar is as much a signal about who the OCC will charter as it is a milestone for Revolut.

But geographic expansion like that is slow and expensive. Three regulators deep, and years from launch. The faster shift is what these same neobanks are doing inside markets they already own: turning from single-product disruptors into full banking platforms.

Inside the region, the ladder-climbing is visible every month.

Mercado Pago expanded insurance to 11 million active customers, with four products through its own digital brokerage. Then it entered private payroll lending.

Nubank added a teen credit card, personal lending in Colombia, NuCel, and full Amazon Brazil checkout presence for NuPay.

Plata reached a $5 billion valuation, with 3.5 million active credit card customers in three years. It added deposits, and it's expanding its product portfolio.

Ualá, the Argentine fintech, raised $195 million from Allianz X, and added insurance.

Nequi extended pre-approved credit to physical point-of-sale terminals for more than 3 million users.

And RecargaPay reached 15 million users, expanding from Pix Financing to credit cards to personal loans. And now it's added Tap-to-Pay.

The pattern is simple. Deeper customer relationships beat isolated product roadmaps.

The end state increasingly resembles a bank, even when the company doesn't start as one. Payments create frequency. Deposits create balances. Lending monetizes the relationship. And adjacent products raise revenue per customer and switching costs. The fintech wedge is becoming an entry point, not the business model itself.

## Chapter: Exits — M&A Is Hot, LatAm IPOs Are Cold

Seventh trend: the exit window.

It's open again, but unevenly. Globally, IPOs and M&A are both accelerating. In Latin America, the recovery is almost entirely an M&A story. Strategic buyers are active. The public-market door is barely open.

The global numbers first. Fintech IPOs rose 50%, to 42, in 2025. Fintech M&A volume climbed from $105 billion in 2023, to $184 billion in 2024, to $251 billion in 2025. And scaled fintechs completed 659 acquisitions in 2025, against 589 by incumbents. That reverses 2024, and, outside 2023, every prior year on record.

The higher bar is the other half. The 30 largest fintech IPOs of the last five years have trailed the broader financial services sector by roughly 24 percentage points in annual total shareholder return. More companies are going public. Fewer of them perform once they do.

In Latin America, those two halves pull apart instead of moving together. The M&A half is real. It's been funding growth here for two years.

Part of the reason is structural. Customer acquisition still runs mostly through paid search and paid social. That channel has gotten more expensive without reliably buying primacy: the deep, repeat relationship that makes someone default to one platform. Buying a company that already has that relationship is often cheaper than building toward it.

The acquisitions have been steady enough to stop making news. Just over the first half of 2026: Celcoin closed its eighth and largest acquisition with Vert in September, after taking Via Capital earlier in the year. Klar, a Mexican neobank, absorbed both Banorte's digital bank Bineo and the mortgage lender Yave inside a single half. Puerto Rico's Evertec, a payments processor, bought the Brazilian banking-software firm Dimensa for $181 million. The Norwegian software group Visma made its fourth Brazilian acquisition in under a year. C6, a Brazilian digital bank, bought the corporate-benefits fintech Alymente. And Nubank, Santander, the banking-as-a-service provider QI Tech, the clearing house Núclea, Tilt, and the SME accounting platform Conta Azul, all in Brazil, bought something in the same stretch.

The IPO half is the one that stays shut. So is the LatAm IPO window actually open? Barely. Nubank trades on the NYSE. Banco Inter trades on Nasdaq. And PicPay joined them on Nasdaq in January 2026. Brazil's first fintech IPO in five years, raising $434 million at a $2.6 billion valuation. Three companies, out of hundreds of scaled fintechs in the region, are closer to confirmation that the window stayed shut for everyone else.

For nearly everyone else, valuations get set in private rounds, as Plata's $5 billion shows. And liquidity comes from strategic buyers and credit and debt structures. Subscale point solutions have exactly one exit: being bought by a platform. Or being merged under pressure, the way the Mexican lenders Kubo and Crédito Maestro were.

## Chapter: Debt Became the Funding Engine — and September Started the Stress Test

Which brings us to the part of this cycle that isn't in the seven trends at all, and might matter most: debt.

Structured credit is a sign of maturity. And in Brazil, the instrument doing most of that work is the FIDC. That's short for Fundo de Investimento em Direitos Creditórios. It's a Brazilian fund that buys a lender's receivables, credit card balances, loan installments, unpaid invoices, and sells shares in that pool to investors. Same basic mechanism as an asset-backed security in the US, or a securitization trust in Canada.

Here's the scale. CloudWalk's $1.1 billion FIDC in the first half was larger than every equity round tracked across the entire half, combined. Add Addi's $150 million J.P. Morgan facility, Clara's $150 million Goldman renewal, 99Pay's $130 million, and Sol Agora's $120 million. Structured debt in the first half of 2026 cleared $2.2 billion, against roughly $1.4 billion of pure equity.

And it kept going. August added Mundi's $150 million facility, plus Kesh, Ume, Presença, and Pinbank. September opened with DM's $74 million across two FIDC series, and Kala's $322 million securitization program, with Gramercy on the senior tranche.

Here's the concern. This is one funding channel, priced off the same curve, funding loan books originated by models trained almost entirely on benign vintages.

Statistical underwriting has been tested before, and it didn't go smoothly. Nu explicitly slowed and repriced its personal-loan book in 2022, when Brazil's benchmark rate spiked, and its 90-plus-day NPL ratio climbed through that cycle. What's different now is the scale, and the gap. Equifax BoaVista data show delinquency among neobank credit-card borrowers nearly tripling, from 7.71% in 2021 to 20.31% in 2025. Over the same years, that same measure fell at traditional banks.

Then the macro. The Fed's first rate hike in three years landed the same week Brazil's central bank cut the Selic for a fifth straight meeting, to 13.75%. A National Confederation of Commerce survey put 82% of Brazilian households in debt, and a third behind on payments, in that same window. Falling policy rates were supposed to ease that pressure. They haven't yet.

Selic at 13.75%, after five straight cuts from a 15% peak, still keeps FIDC funding expensive. A credit event that widens spreads further would reprice every one of those structures at once. And the equity that would normally bridge the gap is the scarcer of the two capital types right now.

So here's my read. Debt has claimed LatAm's funding throne, and it's here to stay. As delinquency keeps climbing, fintech and bank profitability will feel it before founders do. Through higher provisions now, and wider spreads on new FIDC issuance later, even as the policy rate keeps falling. And that pressure pushes capital further toward debt, not away from it. Because equity is having the worse year of the two.

## Chapter: Wrapping Up — Six Calls for the Next Cycle

So let's pull it together. Six takeaways.

First. LatAm is growing through a compounding strategy, but not the fastest one. The region spent this cycle building credit books, licenses, and rails, while the fastest-growing global verticals were trading and deposits. Expect that gap to narrow in 2027, as investment and other financial products land at Nubank, Mercado Pago, Plata, and Inter. And watch whether the region's deposits line starts compounding the way Asia-Pacific's did.

Second. Licenses turned into capital requirements, and that quietly picks winners. Nu México's $1.1 billion injection, the CNBV's Sofipo liquidity phase-in through 2030, and Brazil's VASP capital bar all point the same way. The next two years will thin the field of small regulated entities faster than any competitive dynamic will.

Third. Word of mouth and high NPS will end up shaping what AI answer engines recommend. Nubank's 80% to 90% organic acquisition, and NPS consistently above 80, are exactly the signal a model reads when it summarizes reviews and forums. Reputation stops being a brand metric and becomes training data for who gets recommended.

Fourth. Agentic commerce has enough infrastructure to execute a payment. What's missing is preserving the consumer's authority across time. If a later purchase triggers step-up authentication, the issuer needs a trusted way to verify the agent is still acting within the mandate it was originally given. Yuno, Pomelo, DEUNA, and dLocal each cover part of that chain. But public evidence of the full flow, at meaningful ticket sizes, is still limited. The 2027 milestone is a high-value purchase completed hours or weeks after the instruction, without Face ID, an OTP, or app approval at checkout.

Fifth. Asset tokenization will scale faster than stablecoin payments, and unevenly. Money market instruments, commodity funds, alternatives, and securitized debt are best positioned, precisely where existing infrastructure is already costly, fragmented, or illiquid. Equities and government bonds lag well behind. And B3's own real-pegged stablecoin and tokenized-securities push, live from early 2027, is the region's clearest bet on that exact sequence.

And sixth. LatAm's IPO window stays cold through the next 12 to 18 months, while fintech-led M&A stays hot. Three Nasdaq or NYSE listings out of hundreds of scaled fintechs is not a reopened window. And nothing in the pipeline points to a fourth soon. What keeps moving is consolidation. Scaled fintechs are already out-acquiring incumbents regionally, and names like Celcoin, Klar, Evertec, and C6 will keep buying verticals instead of waiting for an exchange to reopen.

One last thing. October brings the Edu's Newsletter LatAm Fintech Q3 report. Two open questions there. Does Mexico hold its venture-dollar lead over Brazil into the second half? And does the structured-debt wave keep widening, or finally take its first real repricing?
