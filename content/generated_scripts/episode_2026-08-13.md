## Chapter: The Volume Numbers — And Why Headline Figures Lie

Start with the scale, because it reframes everything else.

Brazil received $318.8 billion in on-chain crypto value over the past year. That's close to a third of everything that touched Latin America. Brazil isn't a market within the region — it is the region's crypto market.

BCB president Gabriel Galipolo has said directly that around 90% of that flow is stablecoin movement. Not Bitcoin. Not altcoins. Stablecoins. Since 2019, stablecoins account for roughly $219 billion — 71.7% — of all crypto volume Brazilians have declared to the tax authority. And USDT still owns the corridor: 88.7% of that declared stablecoin flow runs through Tether, even with Circle pushing hard on the institutional side.

Now, the growth rate. Official BCB data shows Brazilians bought $2.63 billion in stablecoins in May 2026, up 158% year over year. June came in at $2.54 billion, versus $1.48 billion the year before. Adoption accelerated straight through the rules tightening around it.

One important caveat before we go further. Headline stablecoin volume overstates real economic usage almost everywhere. Of roughly $35 trillion in global on-chain stablecoin volume, only about $390 billion — close to 1% — represents actual payments. The rest is trading, internal platform transfers, or treasury shuffling. The numbers we're focused on today stay close to genuine end-user activity: trade settlement, consumer dollarization, cross-border shopping. Not raw on-chain totals.

## Chapter: Trade Settlement — The Biggest Segment, and the Least Penetrated

Let's get into the three segments actually moving volume. We'll start with trade settlement, because it's both the largest and the most misunderstood.

The customer here is Brazilian trade-facing SMEs, câmbio brokers, and eFX providers moving payment for goods crossing the border. High frequency, time-sensitive, cost-obsessed. Behaves more like consumer plumbing than corporate finance.

Here's the pain they're solving. A traditional SWIFT wire can take up to five days to settle. And cargo sits at port the whole time, waiting for payment confirmation, generating real demurrage costs on top of the delay. Traditional câmbio adds FX spread and bank fees on top of that, and only clears during banking hours — in two different time zones. Then in 2026, the government added its own layer of dysfunction: a proposed 3.5% IOF tax on stablecoin operations. The industry pushed back. The government shelved it. Then revived a version of it in May through a technical reclassification. That kind of uncertainty pushed volume toward stablecoins rather than away from them. Predictability turned out to matter more than the tax question itself.

The BCB tracks $6.7 billion in total stablecoin operations sent abroad by Brazilians in Q1 2026 — double the same quarter in 2025 — with stablecoins now accounting for 98% of all crypto Brazilians send abroad. Importers, exporters, and câmbio brokers are among the major drivers, cutting costs versus SWIFT. VCX Finance built its entire pitch around this use case: Pix in, USDT or USDC settlement, positioned as the modern alternative to SWIFT, running inside BCB's eFX compliance framework. Transfero runs a parallel play issuing BRZ, its own real-pegged stablecoin, plus Transfero Checkout for merchant settlement.

Now here's where it gets interesting — and where you should stay humble about the upside. Even if every dollar of that $6.7 billion were trade-related, which it isn't, Brazil moves roughly $160 billion a quarter in total goods trade. That puts stablecoin penetration in Brazilian trade settlement at a ceiling of around 4%. The real number is almost certainly lower. Stablecoins have barely scratched Brazilian trade finance. The upside case is a market in the hundreds of billions. Not the low single-digit billions currently moving.

The catch is that Resolution 561 tightens the exact channel — eFX settlement — that's driven this early growth. More on that resolution in a minute. But the legal read from Daniel de Paiva Gomes, a Brazilian crypto regulation lawyer, is that the broader cross-border virtual asset category stays fully operative. Only the narrow eFX-provider-to-foreign-counterparty settlement leg closes. If that migration path holds and stays cheap and fast, this segment has more room to grow than any other one we'll discuss today. If it doesn't, October 1 is where the growth story stalls.

## Chapter: Dollarization — Paying 9 to 10 Points of Yield for Peace of Mind

Second segment: dollarization. Brazilians buying USD exposure even though the math argues against it.

The customer here is Brazilians who want dollar exposure without a foreign bank account, a US address, or a five-figure minimum. Mercado Bitcoin alone logged 40,000 new stablecoin buyers in 2025. Average ticket size up 190%. Concentrated in the 35 to 44 age bracket. That's Brazilians protecting savings, not speculating.

The pain is straightforward: real depreciation and inflation memory make BRL-only savings feel exposed. The traditional fix — a Wise or Nomad-style global account — still means real paperwork and slower onboarding.

Now, here's the cost of protection worth being honest about. Brazil's Selic rate sits at 14%. CDI-linked accounts pay close to that in reais. Every real moved into a dollar position gives up most of that yield. But it protects against currency depreciation in a presidential election year. That gap — 9 to 10 percentage points — is the actual competitive pressure these products are underwriting. It's not a footnote. It's the whole tension.

And yet the segment keeps growing. Here's who's building for it.

ARQ, formerly DollarApp, lets users deposit via Pix and convert straight to USDC — no US address, no SSN, no ITIN required. Approval is automated in most cases. And it pays tiered yield on the USDC balance itself: 2% to 4.5% a year depending on account tier, alongside up to 120% of CDI on any reais left in the account. Nubank offers a comparable rewards program paying roughly 4% a year on USDC holdings, conditioned on minimum balance and active participation. Mercado Pago pays cashback in Meli Dólar — Mercado Livre's own dollar-pegged stablecoin — on credit card purchases for Meli+ subscribers, redeemable at Mercado Livre or sold for reais instantly with no fees. Avenue rolled out USDC to its Brazilian client base in November 2025. OKX Pay and Crypto.com run the same stablecoin account model as an alternative to Wise, Revolut, Nomad, and C6 Global.

The trust anchor the whole category leans on: Circle reported roughly $79 billion in USDC in circulation as of March 2026, audited monthly by Deloitte.

The trend here is up and structurally sticky. Because it's solving a savings problem, not a trading one. And a 9 to 10-point yield gap hasn't been enough to slow it down.

## Chapter: Freelancer Income — Real Demand, Early Days

Third segment, and the smallest of the three: freelancer income.

The customer is Brazilian freelancers and contractors billing foreign clients — directly or through platforms like Upwork and Deel. The pain is simple: wire withdrawal from Upwork to a Brazilian bank carries a flat $30 fee, on top of platform withdrawal fees running 5% to 20%.

The fix: Deel processed $250 million in crypto payouts globally in 2025 and launched formal USDC payroll through its BVNK partnership in 2026. USDC-to-Pix conversion went live in Brazil this year through products like Wayex.

Brazil-specific volume isn't published yet, so this stays a smaller, earlier-stage story for now. But the infrastructure is being built. Watch this space.

## Chapter: Resolution 561 — One Corridor Closed, Not the Market

Now let's talk about the rule that's generating the most noise: Resolution BCB 561.

Published April 30, effective October 1. It rewrites how Brazil's eFX international payment service can settle. From October 1, payments between a Brazilian eFX provider and its foreign counterparty must run exclusively through a registered câmbio operation or a non-resident real account. Stablecoins are explicitly excluded as a settlement instrument in that specific corridor.

Most of the initial coverage overstated the scope. Thiago Amaral, a partner at Barcellos Tucunduva who specializes in payments and crypto, has been direct about what the resolution actually does: it does not ban stablecoins in Brazil. What it does is stop the eFX provider itself from using virtual assets to settle with its foreign counterparty. Client custody of stablecoins stays legal. It's decoupled from the FX operation, not eliminated.

Why now? The stated rationale is traceability and anti-money laundering — and it's not manufactured urgency. Brazil's congressional inquiry into the betting industry, the CPI das Bets, found CPFs belonging to children, deceased people, and falsified data attached to remittances flowing through exactly this kind of unsupervised corridor. Resolution 561 isn't a standalone reaction — it extends a normative process the BCB signaled back in mid-2024. But it lands on top of a real fraud pattern the regulator can point to. Not a hypothetical one.

And here's where you should stay skeptical. This is one piece of a sequence, not an isolated event. Resolutions 519, 520, and 521 built the VASP authorization regime in November 2025. Resolution 561 closes the eFX settlement leg in October. Resolution 580, from July, folds virtual asset providers into the same prudential regime as securities brokerages. And in early August, the BCB added a 24-hour cautionary hold on virtual asset transfers above $10,000 headed to foreign exchanges or self-custodied wallets, effective January 2027. Four resolutions in nine months. That's either a regulator moving with real conviction, or a regulator moving faster than the market can absorb. Depends on who you ask.

Not everyone is convinced it was the right call. Industry associations ABToken and ABCripto came out against the August hold with a sharp technical argument: a fixed-duration hold doesn't distinguish a legitimate transaction from an illicit one. It just delays both equally. And it's trivial to route around by splitting a transfer into smaller pieces. Federal deputy Júlia Zanatta filed a legislative motion in August to suspend that resolution outright. And separately, a payments company called Corpag won a court injunction earlier this year to keep operating after the BCB denied its license — which was enough of a shock to the system that a group of banks and fintechs sent the BCB a joint letter asking the judiciary to defer to the regulator's technical judgment going forward.

None of this has stopped anything yet. All of it tells you the ground under this regulatory push is less settled than a resolution number makes it look.

The deadline that actually matters operationally is October 30. That's when every virtual asset service provider currently running in Brazil has to file for authorization. An estimated 120 providers serve the market today — most without formal licensing. Minimum capital requirements run from roughly $2.1 million to $7.2 million depending on the license category. Foreign firms serving Brazil from offshore shells get 270 days to bring operations onshore. That bar is going to filter the market hard.

## Chapter: The US and Brazil — Same Move, Opposite Directions

While Brazil was tightening one corridor, the largest financial institutions in the world spent June and July racing to own the plumbing outright.

Circle received final approval from the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency on July 10 to run a national trust bank — Circle National Trust — bringing USDC's issuer under direct federal banking supervision for the first time.

Nineteen days earlier, BNY — the world's largest custody bank at $59.4 trillion in assets — made USDC the first stablecoin on its Digital Asset Custody platform. Institutional clients can now mint and redeem directly through BNY rather than routing through Circle separately.

On July 16, Marex began accepting USDC as initial margin collateral for regulated derivatives, with Coinbase providing the custody and conversion infrastructure underneath. Circle's Claire Ching called it collateral moving at internet speed. Coinbase's Liz Martin said she expects the model to extend across more clearinghouses as the market moves toward always-on collateral.

And Samsung, at Galaxy Unpacked on July 22, demoed native stablecoin support inside Samsung Wallet — the app already sitting on 241 million Galaxy phones shipped in 2025. Showing USDC with send, receive, and top-up functions. No launch date, no confirmed issuer partnership, no confirmed country list. Still the largest distribution surface stablecoins have ever been shown on.

Put side by side with Brazil, the instinct is to call these opposite moves. Look closer and it's the same move from two different starting points. Both are institutionalizing the same asset class. The US is pulling stablecoins into the banking system — chartering the issuer, custodying the token at a 240-year-old trust bank, accepting it as derivatives collateral. Brazil is pulling the companies that move stablecoins into a bank-like regulatory perimeter — capital requirements, licensing, AML controls, the same treatment its securities brokerages already carry. Neither government is trying to make stablecoins go away. Both are deciding who gets to be trusted with them.

## Chapter: The $2 to $7 Million Capital Bar — Consolidation Is the Resolution

So what does the October 30 deadline actually mean for the market structure?

Brazil is picking who gets to run the pipes. OKX, Mercado Bitcoin, Bitso, Binance Brazil, dLocal, and Transfero can absorb a $2 to $7 million capital requirement and a compliance build-out. The roughly 120 smaller and informal operators serving today's exporter, freelancer, and remittance flows through side-door eFX arrangements mostly can't. Not by October 30. That's consolidation, and it favors exactly the players already big enough to have shown up in this newsletter before.

dLocal's stablecoin infrastructure lead, Marcelo Dutilh, has framed the broader opportunity this way: emerging markets are where the next wave of digital consumers is coming from, while moving money in and out of those economies stays genuinely complex. Brazil just made that complexity a compliance problem. That's a trade the largest players are built to win, and the smallest are not.

Now here's the case against that clean story.

Every one of these resolutions assumes tighter friction produces more visibility. ABToken and ABCripto's critique cuts at exactly that assumption. A fixed 24-hour hold doesn't catch bad actors — it delays everyone equally. Anyone actually trying to move dirty money splits the transfer below the $10,000 threshold and moves on. Meanwhile the freelancer getting paid by a US client, or the small exporter waiting on a shipment, absorbs the friction with zero corresponding security benefit. Regulation aimed at catching sophisticated bad actors that mainly slows down unsophisticated good ones is a real design failure. Not a rounding error.

There's a second-order risk. Merchant acceptance of stablecoins in Brazil is still low — only 37% of crypto users report having paid in a store with digital assets, despite saying they'd like to, largely because blockchain rails don't talk to legacy point-of-sale systems. That means most of today's stablecoin activity already routes through exchanges and licensed on-ramps where the BCB can see it. Push enough friction into that regulated layer, and some share of volume doesn't disappear. It moves into self-custody and offshore rails the BCB genuinely cannot see. A regulator optimizing for traceability can end up producing less of it. That's the scenario worth watching most closely through 2027.
